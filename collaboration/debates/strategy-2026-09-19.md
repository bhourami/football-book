# Where this project goes next — Claude and Codex, 19 September 2026

Owner's question: *"So what are we doing next. Can you and Codex discuss
the flaws and the mechanics of what we need to do to achieve our aim."*

Stated aim: find genuine, sustained edge. Resources: public data only,
two LLM analysts, manual placement at one UK retail bookmaker, £200 per
book, £10 flat stakes.

Both analysts were asked the same three questions, neither shown the
other's answer, per `PROTOCOL.md`. Both answers are recorded here in
full rather than merged.

---

## What we actually established today

- A fitted Dixon-Coles model, walked forward over 1,180 matches, showed
  **no profitable disagreement with closing prices** at any edge
  threshold. Under the live 3pp rule it would have staked £8,560 and
  lost £787.30 (-9.2%).
- It selected **72.5% of available markets**. A model that finds edge in
  seven of ten matches is not finding edge.
- The earlier qualitative method failed differently: a confirmed
  reasoning error (stale injuries read as a fresh crisis) and a 22.6pp
  "edge" on a 16th-place side against an unbeaten 4th-place side.
- **Codex then found three errors in our own analysis of the above** —
  see the corrections block in `model-v1-backtest.md`. Most importantly:
  what we called CLV is not CLV, and the headline calibration figure was
  arithmetically forced. The conclusion survives; the reasoning had to
  be fixed.

## Codex's position

**Verdict: no demonstrated viable path, and don't budget on finding
one.** Occasional positive-EV transactions are plausible; a durable
forecasting advantage, executable manually at one retail bookmaker and
worthwhile at £10 stakes, is not a credible base case.

Mechanisms that could still produce edge, ranked for *our* resources:

1. **Promotions** — boosts, refunds, free bets. The operator subsidises
   the payoff, so no forecasting skill is required. Best realistic
   prospect; caps and eligibility stop it being a research business.
2. **Patient price comparison** — find when *Ladbrokes specifically* is
   generous against a margin-adjusted consensus of stronger books. Note
   the key property: **this requires no forecasting advantage at all.**
   We don't have to beat the market; we have to notice when one book is
   off it.
3. **One narrow, pre-registered market-residual hypothesis** — e.g. a
   BTTS correction — but only if it adds information *beyond* market
   prices. One frozen experiment, not an open-ended model-tuning loop.
4. **Arbitrage** — mechanically sound, operationally hopeless at £10
   single-bookmaker scale.
5. **News latency** — minutes-to-hours manual placement is far too slow,
   and our Arsenal error showed we're currently bad at judging whether
   information is even new.
6. **A public-data model beating liquid closing prices** — what we just
   tried. Lowest priority; our own results fund no further attempt.

**The decisive argument is economic, not technical.** Even a genuine 3%
ROI on £10 stakes is £0.30 per bet — roughly £3.60/hour at five minutes
a bet, before any research time. And £200 is twenty stakes: variance
can destroy the bankroll even when the edge is real.

On account restrictions, Codex corrected a common overstatement: the
Gambling Commission's 2025 study found commercial restrictions on ~4.31%
of sampled active accounts, and restrictions hit losing accounts too —
so "every winner gets closed immediately" is too strong, but account
lifespan remains genuinely unknown.

## Claude's position

I agree with the verdict and with the ranking. Three things I'd add or
put differently:

**1. The pivot is the whole insight.** Mechanism 2 isn't a consolation
prize, it's a different problem. Everything this project has done so far
asks *"can we predict football better than the market?"* — answered, no.
The price-comparison question asks *"is this particular bookmaker
sometimes wrong relative to every other bookmaker?"* — which needs no
model, no xG, no debate about team news, and is cheap to test on paper
starting immediately. We spent a week trying to be better forecasters
than a market that employs actual quants. We never once checked whether
the shop we're actually betting in is occasionally mispriced.

**2. The economic argument should be separated from the research
question.** Codex is right that £10 stakes make this worthless as an
earner. But those are two different questions and conflating them
forecloses something worth knowing. *"Can a measurable edge be found?"*
is answerable cheaply on paper and is genuinely interesting. *"Is it
worth real money at our stake size?"* is almost certainly no. The
project can honestly pursue the first while conceding the second — it
just has to stop pretending the second is the goal.

**3. I'd weight our own demonstrated failure mode higher than Codex
does.** Both methods didn't merely fail to find edge — they
enthusiastically found edge that wasn't there, in 72.5% of markets, in
one consistent direction on BTTS, and in two confirmed reasoning
errors. Whatever we build next needs a mechanism that makes *finding
nothing* the default output. A price-comparison screen has that
property naturally: most days it should return zero opportunities. That
is a feature, and it's the opposite of everything we've built so far.

## What both analysts agree on

- **Stop staking under the current selection rule.** Neither the
  qualitative nor the fitted version has demonstrated anything that
  justifies risking money. This is a recommendation to the owner, not a
  decision taken here.
- The BTTS pause stays.
- Any future mechanism must be **pre-registered and paper-tested with
  its decision rule fixed in advance**, then judged on executable
  prices, not model probabilities.
- The LLMs' useful role, in Codex's words worth quoting directly:
  *"checking timestamps, reproducing calculations, auditing assumptions
  and finding reasons to reject a proposed edge. They should not turn
  disagreement with the bookmaker into a persuasive betting
  narrative."*

## Proposed next step (owner's call)

Run one pre-registered, **paper-only** price-comparison experiment:
record timestamped Ladbrokes prices against a margin-adjusted consensus
of several other books, on a frozen fixture universe, with the selection
rule and decision threshold written down before any data is collected.
Recheck after a realistic placement delay to see whether generous prices
survive long enough to be taken. Measure opportunity frequency,
disappearance rate, and time spent. No money. Decision rule set in
advance about what result would justify ever staking again.

This tests the one mechanism that fits our actual resources, costs
nothing but time, and has a real chance of returning "no opportunities
exist" — which would be a genuine answer, and the cheapest one we could
possibly buy.
