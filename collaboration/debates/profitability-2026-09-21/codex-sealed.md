# Codex — independent answer, 21 September 2026

Answered without sight of Claude's sealed opening.

---

**1. The biggest lever is price: roughly 3.2–5.1 percentage points of EV recovered by moving these selections to the exchange. It still leaves them losing.**

Using your margin-removed closing probabilities and the three quoted exchange prices, average expected return improves from **−7.8% to approximately −2.7% at 2% commission, or −4.6% at 5%.**

For an isolated back bet, effective decimal odds are `1 + (odds − 1) × (1 − commission)`. Commission applies to net market winnings; multiple positions require market-level accounting. [Betfair’s commission rules](https://support.betfair.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/413/).

That is the most concrete improvement available, already correctly adopted. But a 7% increase in quoted odds does **not** turn −7.8% EV into approximately break-even after commission. All three remain negative against your benchmark.

My verdict: **the current strategy has no demonstrated profitable edge.** Better execution reduces its losses; it does not establish a business. These three observations also cannot establish the exchange improvement across the historical sample.

**2. I see no demonstrated edge exclusive to two AI models.**

The plausible advantage over a human working manually in a spreadsheet is throughput: extracting timestamped lineup and availability information from many unstructured sources, reconciling contradictions, and turning verified changes into reproducible probability adjustments.

The mechanism would be **correct information incorporated into your price before it is fully incorporated into an executable exchange price**. It dies when the market updates first, the information is misinterpreted, the adjustment is poorly calibrated, or available liquidity disappears before a human can act.

Two models could divide extraction and adversarial verification. Their agreement is not independent evidence: they can share sources and errors. Nothing here proves that workflow beats a competent human, much less the market.

I would allow one bounded, paper-only experiment. Freeze its rules before evaluation and compare it with both Dixon–Coles alone and a contemporaneous market baseline on identical fixtures. If the added information does not improve prospective results, remove it.

**3. What we should STOP doing.**

- **Stop treating the 3pp rule as evidence of value.** Selecting 72.5% of markets while losing 9.2% suggests systematic disagreement or miscalibration, not demonstrated selectivity.
- **Stop tuning against the same historical results.** Use those results diagnostically; freeze the next version before its 60 selections.
- **Stop interpreting £35.09 as validation.** Your simulations already explain why it is weak evidence.
- **Stop expanding leagues, model ensembles and narrative analysis without a specific tested contribution.** Lower-division bookmaker margins make that proposed escape route worse.
- **Stop spending AI calls on routine arithmetic and reporting.** Scripts should calculate probabilities, commission, CLV and audit checks. Reserve model calls for genuinely ambiguous information.
- **Stop treating positive median CLV as permission to stake.** Honour the nonpositive-median stopping rule, but recognise that passing it is only a screening result.

Record every qualifying selection prospectively, with executable price, available size, timestamp and a fixed closing benchmark. Report the agreed median CLV alongside commission-adjusted expected return, mean and uncertainty. Median alone can hide economically damaging tails. Keep staking suspended and execution outside the desk.

**4. Where I think Claude is most likely to be wrong.**

I have not seen Claude’s position. My prediction is that the most tempting mistake is **believing exchange pricing plus richer AI analysis closes the remaining gap**.

The execution improvement is measured; the information advantage is conjecture. Adding injuries, tactics and two-model agreement can produce more persuasive explanations without better probabilities.

A second likely mistake is giving “60 selections” more evidential weight than it deserves. It is a useful spending limit, not a certification threshold. Correlated selections reduce its effective information further.

**5. My probability: 30%.**

That is my subjective probability that **the next frozen, prospective 60-selection Premier League exchange run** achieves positive median CLV against a consistently defined closing benchmark. It is a judgment, not a fitted statistical estimate.

“Ever,” if it permits unlimited revisions and repeated windows, mostly measures persistence and selection bias. I would not use that interpretation.

My probability of sustainable profit after commission, execution friction and operating costs is lower. **This may simply not be profitably salvageable. The next experiment should earn continued investment, not presume it.**
